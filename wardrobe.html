A LOOK INSIDE THE PROCESS

The field notes.

What I explored, what I built, and what changed along the way.

NOTE 01

The project & the problem

For my PLE project, I built a mobile wardrobe app for people with ADHD. The problem I wanted to work on was clothing chaos: having a wardrobe full of items, losing track of what is there and finding it difficult to decide what to keep.

The app brings clothing photos, wear history and a guided decluttering flow together. I worked on the research, interaction design and implementation, using React Native, Expo Router, TypeScript and Supabase.

THE PROJECT QUESTION

How could a clothing app help people with ADHD get an overview of their wardrobe and make smaller, more manageable decluttering decisions?

NOTE 02

Research before features

I carried out a survey and two interviews with people with ADHD to explore the problem from the users’ perspective. That research gave the project a user-centred starting point, rather than beginning with a list of technical features.

I used the research and design process to consider what the app should help people do: see what they own, understand what they actually wear and work through items they might want to let go of.

The screenshots below show the resulting interface and some earlier iterations. The survey and interviews were part of the research process; these interface images show the implementation rather than the underlying research results.

NOTE 03

From outfit planning to decluttering

The direction changed during the project. The focus moved from outfit planning towards wardrobe overview and decluttering support. That gave the app a clearer central task: helping someone work through the clothing they already have.

Wear tracking supports that task. Instead of choosing items arbitrarily, the app can bring less-worn clothing to the user’s attention. Suggestions are based on registered wear data, so their usefulness depends on what the user has logged.

NOTE 04

A walk through the app

  1. Register clothing. Add items to a visual wardrobe and organise them by category and weather.
  2. Log what gets worn. Record wear history and see when and how often an item was used.
  3. Start a declutter session. Choose Warm, Koud or Hele jaar and review less-worn items.
  4. Make a decision. Keep an item or mark it for removal. Marked items are collected in a declutter basket and identified in the wardrobe.
  5. Review and take the next step. A final overview leads to advice such as donation, a clothing swap, a thrift shop or textile recycling.

The interface is in Dutch. The wardrobe also includes category and weather filters, and the clothing detail screen shows monthly wear statistics. A supporting outfit suggestion screen uses separate top and bottom carousels to bring less-worn clothing back into view.

NOTE 05

Refining the decisions

The flow developed through several versions. Early screens used three decisions — remove, doubt and keep — and grouped them in a session summary. I later removed the “twijfel” option from the decision screen, leaving the two immediate actions: keep or remove. The screenshots below include those earlier screens as well as the later basket.

The basket makes it possible to see the items marked for removal together. The advice page gives the flow an ending beyond simply selecting clothing. Those changes help connect the individual decisions to an overview and a next step.

I refined the weather filtering and screen transitions, and adjusted spacing and buttons as the app developed.

NOTE 06

The data behind the wardrobe

I used Supabase for the app’s data, with clothing records and separate wear logs. Logging a wear updates the item’s last-worn date and wear count, and records the wear event. That history supports both the least-worn selection and the monthly chart.

React Native and Expo Router provided the mobile screens and navigation, while TypeScript helped organise the data and component interfaces. Supabase also supported accounts and image storage.

SMALL DETAIL, IMPORTANT BEHAVIOUR

“Hele jaar” is a distinct weather label. Getting weather labels and filtering right matters because it changes which clothing appears in a session.

NOTE 07

Testing & reflection

I wrote Jest tests for weather label normalisation, least-worn filtering and declutter selection logic. These checks focus on the rules behind the interface: which items are selected and how labels are interpreted.

The project also involved preparing a demo, reflection, a Business Model Canvas and marketing material. That broadened the work beyond implementation to explaining the idea, its audience and its value.

The working app shows how research, interaction choices and data can fit together. Automated checks verify parts of its behaviour; they do not establish that it reduces decision fatigue. The screenshots document the interface and its iterations; they do not establish an effect on users’ decision fatigue.
